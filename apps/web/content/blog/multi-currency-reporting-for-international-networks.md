When a network opens its first location in another country, a familiar spreadsheet appears: one column of revenue figures, some in Canadian dollars, some in pounds, some in euros, added up into a single "network total". That number is wrong, and decisions made from it can be expensive.

This article explains the problem and three honest alternatives. None of them is complicated; they just need to be chosen deliberately.

## Why a mixed total is misleading

Suppose a network has three locations this month:

| Location | Revenue |
|---|---|
| Toronto | CA$48,000 |
| London | £31,000 |
| Berlin | €36,000 |

Adding 48,000 + 31,000 + 36,000 gives 115,000 of nothing in particular. The units differ, so the sum has no meaning. Worse, if the pound strengthens next month, London's real performance can look better or worse purely because of exchange rates.

The same applies to royalties, marketing fund contributions, average order value and anything else measured in money.

## Option 1: report each currency separately

The simplest honest report shows a total per currency:

- CAD: 48,000
- GBP: 31,000
- EUR: 36,000

This is exact and needs no assumptions. It works well for operational reporting, comparing locations that share a currency, and anything that will be invoiced or paid in that currency. Its limitation is obvious: you can't see the network as a single number.

## Option 2: convert with a stated rate and method

When you need one number — for a board report, a budget or group accounts — convert explicitly and say how. Common conventions, consistent with how accounting standards such as IAS 21 treat foreign currency:

- **Average rate for the period** for revenue and costs (income-statement items).
- **Closing rate at the period end** for balances such as cash, receivables and amounts owed by franchisees.
- **A single source** for rates (your bank, a central bank reference rate or your accounting system), recorded alongside the report.

Always show the reporting currency, the rate source and the date or period. Keep the original amounts available underneath, so anyone can check the conversion.

## Option 3: constant-currency comparisons

To understand growth, compare this period with last year using the *same* exchange rates for both. This removes currency movements from the comparison, so you see whether locations actually sold more. Public companies often report "constant currency" growth for exactly this reason.

A practical approach: convert both periods at last year's average rates, report the growth, and show the currency effect as a separate line.

## Practical rules for a network

1. **Store money with its currency.** Every amount should carry its currency code and be stored in the currency's minor unit (cents, pence) — never as a bare number.
2. **Invoice in one currency per invoice.** Royalties and fees for a location are invoiced in the agreed currency. Don't mix currencies on one invoice.
3. **Agree the royalty currency in the franchise agreement.** Is the royalty calculated on local revenue and paid in local currency, or converted? If converted, at which rate and when?
4. **Separate operational and financial views.** Locations need their own currency. Headquarters may need a converted view. Label both clearly.
5. **Disclose, don't hide.** If a dashboard shows a converted total, put the method one click away.
