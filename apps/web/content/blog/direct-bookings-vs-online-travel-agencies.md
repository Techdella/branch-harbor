For most independent hotels, B&Bs and small groups, online travel agencies (OTAs) are both the biggest source of bookings and the biggest single cost of selling a room. Commission rates vary by agency, market and contract, and are commonly quoted in the range of roughly 15–25% of the room revenue. That's a large share of a night's margin.

The answer is rarely "leave the OTAs". They bring guests who would never have found you, especially for first stays, midweek nights and international travellers. The goal is a healthier mix: let OTAs do what they're good at, and make sure guests who already want to book with you directly can do so easily.

## Know your real cost per booking

Before changing anything, measure. For each channel, estimate the cost of acquiring a booking:

| Channel | What it costs | What to include |
|---|---|---|
| Online travel agency | Commission on room revenue | Commission, any visibility or promotion programmes, payment fees if the OTA doesn't collect |
| Direct website | Marketing and technology | Booking engine, payment processing fees, search and social ads, your time |
| Phone and email | Staff time | Time spent quoting and confirming, payment handling |
| Repeat guests | Retention | Email tools, loyalty perks |

Compare channels on cost per *stay*, not per click. A direct booking from paid search can cost more than an OTA booking if the ads are poorly targeted.

## Fix the direct booking path first

Many guests find a property on an OTA and then look for its own website. If the website can't take a booking with a deposit, shows different prices, or sends them to an email form, they go back and book on the OTA. Check these basics:

1. **Live availability and rates** on your own site, from the same inventory the front desk uses, so the last room can't be sold twice.
2. **A clear total before payment**: room rate, taxes, deposit and cancellation terms shown before the guest enters card details.
3. **A short checkout** on mobile — dates, room, guest details, payment. Ask for registration details later if the law requires them.
4. **A confirmation and a manage-booking link**, so guests can change dates or cancel without phoning.
5. **Fast pages** that work on a phone in a train station.

## Give a reason to book direct — within your contracts

Rate parity clauses — contract terms requiring a hotel not to offer lower prices elsewhere — have been restricted in several European countries, and the EU's Digital Markets Act places obligations on the largest platforms that affect them. Rules differ by country and by contract, so read your agreements and take advice before changing your pricing strategy.

Even where price parity applies, you can usually add value instead of cutting price: flexible cancellation, breakfast, late check-out, a welcome drink, a room upgrade when available, or perks for returning guests. Make these visible on your own site.

## Use OTAs for reach, then earn the next stay

A guest who booked through an OTA is still your guest when they arrive. With their consent, invite them to your newsletter or to book directly next time, and make sure the stay itself is memorable. Respect the OTA's rules about contacting guests, and only send marketing to people who opted in.

## Keep inventory in one place

The more channels you sell through, the more important a single source of truth for rooms becomes. Whether you use a channel manager or not, every sale — direct, OTA, phone, walk-in — should reduce the same room count in the same moment. Overbooking is the most expensive mistake in distribution: it costs a relocated guest, a bad review and often a commission anyway.

## Measure monthly

Track, per property and per currency:

- Share of room nights by channel.
- Average daily rate (ADR) by channel.
- Cost per stay by channel.
- Cancellation rate by channel.
- Repeat guest share.

Move the mix gradually. A few percentage points of room nights shifted from OTAs to direct bookings each year is a realistic and valuable goal.
