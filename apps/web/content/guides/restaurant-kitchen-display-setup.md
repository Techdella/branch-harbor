A kitchen display system (KDS) replaces printed tickets with screens that show each order to the people who need to cook it. Done well, it reduces lost tickets, shows how long orders have been waiting and makes busy periods calmer. Done badly, it puts a slow screen in a hot, loud room. This guide covers the decisions that make the difference.

## 1. Map your stations

List every place food is prepared: grill, fryer, cold section, pizza oven, bar, pastry. Then decide:

- **Which items go to which station.** A burger with fries goes to grill *and* fryer; each station sees only its part.
- **Whether you need an expo (pass) screen.** The expediter sees the whole order and confirms it's complete before it leaves the kitchen.
- **Which station owns modifiers** like "no onions" or "gluten-free bun". The station that makes that component must see it clearly.

Draw the kitchen layout and place screens where cooks can see them without turning away from their work.

## 2. Choose the hardware

- **Screens:** at least 15" for most stations, mounted at eye level, bright enough for a well-lit kitchen. Commercial touchscreens or tablets in protective enclosures both work.
- **Input:** touch is fine for most kitchens; wet or gloved hands may prefer a physical bump bar.
- **Mounting:** away from direct heat and steam; cables protected.
- **Network:** a reliable wired connection or strong Wi-Fi dedicated to operations. Test signal strength at every station.

## 3. Design the ticket

Keep tickets readable at a glance:

- Large item names, quantities first.
- Modifiers and allergy notes highlighted, never truncated.
- Order type (dine-in, pickup, delivery) and table or pickup name.
- A timer showing how long the ticket has been open.
- Colour changes as the ticket ages (for example after 8 and 12 minutes), plus a text or icon cue — not colour alone.

## 4. Agree the flow

A typical flow per ticket at each station:

1. **New** — the ticket appears, with an optional chime.
2. **Started** — the cook taps when they begin, so others know it's in progress.
3. **Bumped** — the cook marks their part done; it leaves their screen.
4. **Recalled** — a mistaken bump can be brought back.

At the expo screen, the order is complete when every station has bumped its part. Decide who can recall tickets and how long a bumped ticket remains recallable.

## 5. Set prep times and pacing

Record a target prep time for each item or station. Use it to:

- Show which tickets are running late.
- Quote realistic pickup times to customers.
- Spot items that consistently take longer than planned.

If you take online orders, consider pausing or extending pickup times automatically when the kitchen is overloaded, rather than letting quotes become promises you can't keep.

## 6. Plan for failure

Screens and networks fail. Before you go live:

- Keep a printer configured as a fallback for at least one station.
- Know what happens to new orders if a screen loses connection — and how the screen shows that it's out of date.
- Write a one-page "if the KDS is down" procedure and keep it in the kitchen.

## 7. Train and launch

- Run a full service on the KDS during a quiet period, with paper as backup.
- Ask cooks what's hard to read and fix the layout before going fully paperless.
- Review timers and late tickets after the first week.
